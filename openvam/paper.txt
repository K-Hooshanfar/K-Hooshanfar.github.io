abstract
Predicting human gaze is a core capability for applications ranging from web/UI design analysis to robotics and human-computer interaction. Yet, most visual attention modeling methods output only a dense saliency map, which is often insufficient for action: practitioners need to connect attention peaks to discrete elements in the scene (what) and understand the drivers of those peaks in context (why), while remaining robust to domain shift across natural images, commercial content, and UI/web layouts. We, therefore, introduce OpenVAM (Open-world Visual Attention Modeling with VLMs), a unified framework that jointly addresses universality and explainability across heterogeneous domains (natural scenes, commercial imagery, and UI/web layouts) and supervision modalities. OpenVAM adopts a decoupled-but-aligned design: a dedicated dense visual pathway provides stable, spatially precise localization, while an instruction-following vision--language semantic head generates grounded what/why explanations conditioned on the same image and data-type prompts. A three-stage training strategy preserves strong localization priors while progressively introducing language grounding and improving explanation alignment via parameter-efficient adaptation without perturbing the saliency branch. We further propose a scalable pipeline to generate multi-domain saliency-reason annotations for training and systematic evaluation. Experiments across diverse datasets show that OpenVAM improves robustness under domain shift while producing image-grounded explanations that make saliency predictions more interpretable.

# Introduction

Visual attention selectively prioritizes processing toward particular locations, features, or objects in a scene~. Modeling this process aims to predict this selection from visual input, typically by estimating the spatial distribution of human gaze over an image~. This is commonly formulated as saliency prediction: given an image, produce a dense saliency map whose values approximate the probability (or density) of human fixations at each location~. Saliency maps are useful because they provide a compact, interpretable summary of where people are likely to look, enabling applications such as optimizing marketing and e-commerce layouts~, prioritizing content in web pages and user interfaces (UIs)~, improving media understanding and compression~, and supporting robotics and human-computer interaction systems that need to allocate sensing or computation to the most informative regions~.

Despite the utility of saliency maps, a heatmap alone is often insufficient for downstream decisions~. In practice, users must (i) link attention peaks to discrete elements in the scene, such as a headline, logo, face, product image, or call-to-action button, rather than to pixel locations, and (ii) identify the reasons that drive those peaks in context, such as semantic relevance, size and position, color/contrast, typography, or layout conventions. Accordingly, we frame practical attention modeling around three complementary questions: (1) where observers look, (2) what elements account for the predicted attention, and (3) why these elements draw attention in context. For instance, when a landing page exhibits a strong attention peak in the top-left region, improving the design depends on whether that peak is caused by a prominent headline, a bright call-to-action button, a distracting icon, or an unintended contrast imbalance~.

Most saliency predictors still focus on the where question. Most models commonly adopt an encoder–decoder architecture~, and with strong backbones and large-scale training can perform well on in-domain benchmarks~. However, deployment is limited by the inherently multi-domain nature of saliency: attention cues vary across natural scenes, commercial imagery, and UI/web layouts, and supervision signals exhibit distinct biases (e.g., data acquisitions using eye vs.\ mouse tracking)~. As a result, models trained on a single dataset or visual context often degrade under domain shift, and it is unclear which specialized model to apply to previously unseen images. Recent work has trained unified saliency models across datasets and modalities~. SUM~ conditions the predictor on the input domain (e.g., natural images, commercial imagery, or UI/web layouts) and the data acquisition type to support a single model across heterogeneous visual contexts. However, these approaches typically still output only a saliency map and may rely on explicit domain identifiers at inference, which can be unreliable for mixed, ambiguous, or open-world inputs.

Progress toward universal, explainable attention modeling is also limited by data. Standard saliency benchmarks provide fixation points or fixation-density maps, but rarely include aligned natural-language rationales or explicit descriptions of attended elements~. Scaling such annotations is difficult: explanations are costly to collect and inherently ambiguous because attention may reflect low-level cues (e.g., contrast or edges), high-level semantics (e.g., faces or text), and domain-specific conventions (e.g., UI layout patterns or brand placement). Consequently, there is no widely adopted multi-domain benchmark that pairs saliency supervision with free-form, image-grounded explanations.

In this work, we introduce OpenVAM: Open-World Visual Attention Modeling with VLMs, a unified framework designed to address universality and explainability jointly. OpenVAM couples a dedicated encoder-decoder saliency predictor for accurate dense estimation with a VLM-based semantic head that produces grounded, concise explanations. Crucially, OpenVAM uses a decoupled-but-aligned design: dense localization is learned in a stable visual pathway, while language is trained to explain the prediction without destabilizing spatial precision. This enables a single system to output a saliency map (where) together with open-vocabulary descriptions of salient elements (what) and short natural-language rationales (why) within a unified framework. We further release a new multi-domain dataset that augments existing saliency benchmarks with saliency reason annotations, image-grounded explanations describing the salient elements and the cues that drive attention, spanning natural scenes, commercial imagery, and UI/web layouts. This dataset enables systematic training and evaluation of models that must generalize across domains while producing actionable where, what, and why outputs. Our contributions are as follows:

# Related Works

Saliency Prediction.
Early saliency models drew inspiration from human vision and primarily focused on bottom-up mechanisms, where attention is driven by local contrast and contextual distinctiveness~. With the emergence of large-scale gaze and saliency datasets~, learning-based methods quickly became dominant, as deep predictors began to directly regress fixation-density (saliency) maps and improved generalization by leveraging large-scale supervision and pretrained visual representations~. Recent models typically employ multi-scale or encoder-decoder architectures with pretrained backbones, and increasingly rely on attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies and global context~. Saliency prediction now extends beyond natural images to ads, e-commerce, and UI or web layouts, whose differing semantics and structure induce domain-specific attention cues and annotations~. However, most existing approaches are not universal: they are typically developed and evaluated within a single domain, and their performance often degrades when applied to new content distributions.

Unified Models.
Unified saliency modeling aims to train a single predictor across heterogeneous datasets and visual contexts, reducing the need to maintain specialized models per domain. Prior work, such as UNISAL, leverages domain adaptation to combine multiple saliency domains within one framework~, while UniAR explores large-scale unified training with multimodal transformers to capture diverse attention behaviors across tasks and content~. SUM explicitly targets cross-domain saliency by integrating efficient long-range modeling with a U-Net-style predictor and conditioning the network on the input domain (e.g., natural, UI/web, commercial) to adapt its behavior within one model~. Although effective, these unified approaches remain centered on predicting saliency heatmaps, and they do not provide element-level grounding or explanations that connect peaks to what is being attended and why.

VLMs in Visual Attention Modeling.
Recent work has explored using VLMs to make attention predictions more interpretable, by attaching natural-language descriptions to gaze behavior rather than producing heatmaps alone. XSal~ is an example that uses a general-purpose VLM to generate semantic proposals, maps them to image regions via a segmentation model, applies contextual prioritization to select the top-K prototypes, and predicts a focused saliency map with a separate saliency head. While XSal improves interpretability, it is not end-to-end, does not specialize the VLM for saliency learning, and is developed primarily on natural-image settings for question answering, which limits robustness under domain and modality shift. GazeXplain~ annotates fixation-level text and proposes an attention-language decoder that jointly predicts scanpaths (the temporal sequence of fixations) and generates explanations, supported by semantic alignment and cross-dataset co-training for generalization. While complementary, this line of work targets dynamic eye-movement trajectories and task-driven viewing, whereas our focus is dense, image-wide saliency across heterogeneous domains (e.g., natural images, commercial imagery, and UI/web layouts) with grounded where, what, and why outputs. Another related direction uses gaze to guide VLM computation: GazeVLM~ leverages human gaze as an external supervisory signal to select regions-of-interest and prune visual tokens for more efficient VLM inference. This setting assumes gaze is available at inference and optimizes efficiency, and thus is not directly applicable to open-world saliency prediction where gaze is unknown and must be inferred from the image.

# Method: OpenVAM

Human visual attention is inherently dense and spatial: saliency supervision provides pixel-level targets that are highly sensitive to local structure. In contrast, language supervision is sparse and semantic: a short explanation typically refers to a few concepts and relationships rather than precise spatial layouts. Directly fine-tuning a VLM end-to-end to satisfy both objectives leads to undesirable coupling: the dense prediction quality becomes sensitive to prompt phrasing and language-head training dynamics, and the model may sacrifice spatial precision to optimize token likelihood (see ablations). OpenVAM resolves this mismatch with a decoupled-but-aligned design. We explicitly separate where attention is (dense saliency) from what/why it is (text explanation), while forcing both heads to condition on the same input image and dataset context. Concretely, OpenVAM (i) anchors dense attention localization in a stable, domain-agnostic visual backbone with a dedicated dense decoder, (ii) augments it with a VLM-based semantic head that produces grounded explanations and improves cross-domain generalization, and (iii) adopts a staged training strategy that preserves strong saliency priors while gradually introducing language conditioning.

## Model Architecture

As demonstrated in~, given an image I^H W 3, OpenVAM outputs a saliency map S^H W and an explanation sequence Y=\y_t\_t=1^T, where T denotes the generated token length. OpenVAM consists of a DINOv3-based~ visual encoder, a coarse-to-fine saliency decoder, and an instruction-following vision-language module for attention grounding and explanation. The central design principle is decoupled but aligned learning: dense attention localization is learned with a specialized visual pathway, while semantic grounding is learned with a language pathway that conditions on the same image and a data-type prompt, and the two are optimized jointly.

Visual Encoder.

We decouple dense saliency prediction from the VLM’s native vision tower and extract multi-level features from a DINOv3~ ViT by tapping hidden states at layers \(\3,6,9,12\\). This choice captures complementary information across depth: earlier blocks preserve fine spatial detail and local contrast cues that are critical for precise fixation localization, whereas deeper blocks encode higher-level semantics and broader context useful for resolving what attracts attention. Using (\(L_3, L_6, L_9\)) blocks provides a compact yet effective hierarchy, and using \(L_12\) for cross-modal fusion leverages the most semantically abstract representation for alignment with text without entangling the saliency features with language dynamics. Vision-Language Semantic Head.

OpenVAM uses an instruction-following Qwen-VL~ model to generate a concise explanation conditioned on the same image. Visual tokens are incorporated into the language context via a lightweight adapter. The language model generates:~Y p_\!(Y \,|\, I, (Data-type Instruction)), where () is a data-type instruction template (e.g., natural scene, web/UI, document). This explicit data-type conditioning encourages consistent explanation style and grounding across domains. Importantly, the VLM is not the primary source of dense spatial features; it functions as an auxiliary semantic head that improves interpretability and promotes domain-robust representations without destabilizing saliency learning.

Both heads operate on the same image and are optimized jointly. The saliency head is trained with dense saliency supervision to localize attention accurately, while the language head is trained to produce grounded descriptions of salient regions. This joint training couples spatial and semantic supervision without forcing the dense predictor to rely on VLM-native visual features, which mitigates prompt sensitivity and stabilizes learning across heterogeneous data.

## Training Procedure

As illustrated in~, we train OpenVAM with three stages designed to preserve a strong saliency prior while progressively introducing language conditioning and improving explanation alignment.

Stage I.
We first learn a strong saliency localization model using saliency supervision only. Specifically, we train the visual encoder with the coarse-to-fine saliency decoder to predict fixation-derived saliency maps. This stage is crucial to capture robust, spatially precise attention priors without any language modeling component, and serves as the initialization for subsequent stages.

Stage II.
Starting from the Stage~I model, we attach an instruction-following Qwen-VL generator as a semantic head and condition it with a data-type instruction.
Crucially, we freeze the language backbone and train only the components responsible for (i) dense localization and (ii) injecting visual information into the language context.
Concretely, we optimize the DINOv3 encoder, the multi-scale dense decoder, and the lightweight vision-to-language adapter modules.
This stage introduces text conditioning and encourages semantic grounding without destabilizing the language model or entangling token-level optimization with dense saliency prediction.

Stage III.
To improve explanation and consistency without perturbing localization, we freeze the saliency pathway (visual encoder and dense decoder) and optimize only the VLM side: we keep the visual adapter trainable and apply LoRA~ to the language transformer, the LM head, and required norms. This parameter-efficient adaptation sharpens grounded descriptions while preserving the localization behavior learned in Stages~I--II.

## Loss Functions

We train OpenVAM with a composite saliency objective inspired by prior saliency works~. The objective combines complementary terms that capture both distributional agreement and structural consistency between the predicted saliency map and human attention signals. Let S^g denote the ground-truth saliency map, F^g the ground-truth fixation map, and S the predicted saliency map. Our saliency loss is

L_sal &=
_1\,L_KL(S^g,S)
+_2\,L_CC(S^g,S)
+_3\,L_SIM(S^g,S) \\
&+_4\,L_NSS(F^g,S)
+_5\,L_MSE(S^g,S) \, .

where _i is a scaling factor. Following common practice, we minimize dissimilarity terms (KL, MSE) and maximize similarity terms (CC, SIM, NSS). We summarize the saliency loss terms, reporting the formulation of each term, what it measures, and its role in optimizing OpenVAM in the supplementary material.

Stage-wise objectives.
Stage I learns a strong localization prior by optimizing the saliency pathway only:
L^(I)=L_sal. Stage II continues saliency training under the VLM representation space; the LM backbone remains frozen, and we update the visual pathway: L^(II)=L_sal. Stage III optimizes the language objective for the explanation head, while keeping L_sal as a fixed localization constraint (no gradients flow into the frozen saliency branch): L^(III)=\,L_sal+\,L_text. L_text is the standard autoregressive token-level cross-entropy between the generated explanation and the ground-truth text.

We build a unified multi-domain saliency and reason corpus by augmenting six established saliency benchmarks with image-grounded textual rationales. Each image is paired with a concise explanation in the form Object (location): reason, which complements dense saliency supervision (where) with aligned what and why descriptions.

## Experimental Settings

Evaluation Metrics.
Following prior works, we evaluate saliency performance using location-based and distribution-based metrics~. Location-based metrics include NSS (Normalized Scan-path Saliency) and AUC-Judd, which compare predictions to a binary fixation map and emphasize salient locations. Distribution-based metrics include CC (Correlation Coefficient), SIM (Similarity), and KLD (Kullback-Leibler Divergence), which use a grayscale saliency map to measure how closely the predicted and ground-truth saliency distributions match. Higher scores indicate better performance for all metrics except KLD, where values closer to zero are better. For explanation quality, we use GPT-4.1~ to score semantic alignment between generated explanations and ground truth as JScore, and we also report ROUGE-1/2/L~ and BLEU-1/2/3/4~. JScore details are in the supplementary material.

Implementation Details. We implement OpenVAM in PyTorch and train on a single NVIDIA L40 GPU. Following~, we resize input images and the corresponding saliency and fixation maps to 256256. We use DINOv3 ViT-B/16~ as the visual encoder, and adopt the Qwen PatchMerger as the visual adapter. For Stage~III, we apply LoRA to the language transformer with rank r=16, scaling =32, and dropout 0.05. We also apply LoRA to the LM head (r=16, =16). OpenVAM-3B and 7B are based on Qwen2.5-VL-3B~ and 7B, respectively. OpenVAM-4B and 8B are based on Qwen3-VL-4B~ and 8B, respectively. Please see the supplement for additional implementation details.

Overall, OpenVAM ranks first across most datasets and metrics, indicating closer agreement with human attention in both distribution and structure.

## Ablation Studies

Effect of Training Strategy. ~ compares our proposed stage-wise optimization to a joint training baseline that trains all modules used across Stages~I--III simultaneously in a single run. Joint training performs poorly in practice, highlighting the core challenge in OpenVAM: dense, spatial saliency supervision and sparse, semantic language supervision have incompatible training dynamics when directly coupled end-to-end. In contrast, our stage-wise strategy reliably yields strong saliency and text-generation metrics across datasets. Stage-wise training consistently improves both localization and explanation quality, suggesting that isolating saliency learning before introducing and then adapting language supervision preserves strong spatial priors.

Effect of Training Stages.~ evaluates our staged training strategy, which is designed to reconcile the mismatch between dense, spatial saliency supervision and sparse, semantic language supervision. Stage~I trains only the dedicated visual pathway, establishing a strong localization prior. Stage~II then introduces the semantic head while keeping the language backbone frozen, so saliency improves under text conditioning. Importantly, moving from Stage~II to Stage~III further improves performance: by freezing the entire saliency pathway and applying LoRA only on the language side and training the visual adapter, Stage~III strengthens cross-modal alignment and explanation fidelity without perturbing the dense predictor. This aligns with our design goal of being decoupled but aligned: we refine semantics and grounding while preserving the overall model behavior compared to Stage~II.

Effect of Visual Backbone.
Table~ highlights the impact of the visual backbone on attention localization, and we evaluate this effect under Stage~I only to isolate the learned saliency prior before any language supervision is introduced. When we replace DINOv3 with the original Qwen ViT, the saliency metrics drop noticeably, indicating that Qwen ViT provides a weaker localization prior to dense prediction. In contrast, DINOv3 consistently yields stronger spatial cues, which better support our Stage~I objective of learning a stable where pathway before introducing language supervision. This finding motivates anchoring OpenVAM’s dense saliency branch with DINOv3, while using Qwen as an auxiliary semantic head for what/why grounding.

# Discussion

# Conclusion

We presented OpenVAM, a unified framework for open-world visual attention modeling that outputs both a dense saliency map (where) and grounded natural-language rationales (what/why). OpenVAM addresses the fundamental mismatch between dense spatial supervision and sparse semantic supervision via a decoupled-but-aligned design: a dedicated encoder--decoder provides stable, domain-agnostic localization, while an instruction-following VLM head generates explanations without destabilizing the dense predictor. A three-stage training strategy further preserves strong saliency priors while progressively improving cross-modal grounding through parameter-efficient language adaptation. Experiments across natural imagery, e-commerce, and UI/web layouts demonstrate that OpenVAM achieves strong multi-domain saliency performance and image-grounded explanations that improve interpretability under domain shift.
